MERIDIAN SYNC SERVICE — OPERATIONS HANDBOOK
Version 4.2 — Last updated 2026-08-30
Owner: Platform Reliability

================================================================
1. PURPOSE AND SCOPE
================================================================

Meridian Sync is the background service that keeps the primary datastore and the
read replicas consistent. It is not a request-path service: user-facing latency
does not depend on it directly, but when it falls behind, the replicas serve
stale data and dashboards disagree with each other.

This handbook is written for the on-call engineer. It assumes you can reach the
admin CLI and read the service log. It does not cover the client SDK.

================================================================
2. ARCHITECTURE IN ONE PARAGRAPH
================================================================

A single coordinator process owns the change stream and hands ordered batches to
a pool of worker processes. Each worker applies a batch to one replica and
reports the highest sequence number it has durably written. The coordinator
tracks the slowest worker; the gap between that worker and the head of the
stream is the replication lag reported on the dashboard.

State lives in three places: the change stream (durable, append-only), a small
checkpoint file per replica, and an in-memory queue in the coordinator. Only the
change stream is a source of truth. If the coordinator restarts, it rebuilds
the in-memory queue from the checkpoint and the stream; nothing is lost.

================================================================
3. CAPACITY AND BACKPRESSURE
================================================================

The coordinator reads the change stream as fast as it can and pushes batches
into a bounded queue. When the queue is full it stops reading. That is the
backpressure signal: the read position in the stream stops advancing while the
write position keeps moving.

Sustained backpressure means the workers cannot keep up with the write rate of
the primary. The earliest sign is not an error — it is rising replication lag
with a healthy CPU profile, because the workers are waiting on disk, not
computing. Do not add workers in that situation; the bottleneck is the replica's
storage, and more workers make the contention worse.

The service is sized so that a single worker can absorb roughly 1.8 times the
observed peak write rate. That headroom is deliberate: it is what lets a replica
fall offline for maintenance without the lag becoming unbounded.

================================================================
4. COMMON ERRORS AND WHAT THEY MEAN
================================================================

ERR_CONN_4421
  Raised when the coordinator cannot establish a connection to a replica within
  the connect timeout (default 5s). Almost always a network or firewall change,
  not a database problem. Check that the replica's listener port is reachable
  from the coordinator host before touching the service.

ERR_CONN_4422
  The connection was established but the handshake did not complete. Usually a
  certificate that expired on the replica side. Rotate the certificate and
  restart the replica.

ERR_AUTH_1180
  Authentication to the replica failed. This is a credential problem: the
  service account password was rotated without updating the secret the
  coordinator reads. It is not related to ERR_CONN_4421 despite the similar
  prefix.

ERR_QUEUE_7003
  The coordinator's queue reached its high-water mark and it stopped reading the
  change stream. This is backpressure working as designed, not a fault. It only
  becomes a fault when it persists for more than ten minutes, which means the
  workers are genuinely saturated.

ERR_APPLY_5510
  A worker failed to apply a batch because a replica rejected a write (for
  example, a constraint violation introduced by a schema change). The worker
  parks the batch and retries with exponential backoff. If it does not clear,
  the batch is written to the dead-letter file and the worker moves on.

================================================================
5. DIAGNOSING REPLICATION LAG
================================================================

Lag is the distance between the newest change in the stream and the newest
change a replica has applied. It is reported in seconds, but it is really a
count of pending changes divided by the apply rate — which is why a burst of
small writes can show a scary-looking lag number that clears on its own.

To find the cause, work outward from the coordinator:

  1. If the queue is empty and lag is high, the coordinator is not reading fast
     enough. Check its CPU and the stream read latency.
  2. If the queue is full and lag is high, the workers are the bottleneck. Look
     at replica disk latency first, then at the apply rate per worker.
  3. If lag is high but the queue depth is normal and CPU is low, suspect a
     clock problem on the replica. The lag is computed from timestamps and a
     skewed clock reads as lag that never resolves.

The single most useful command is the per-worker apply rate over the last five
minutes; comparing workers isolates whether the problem is one replica or all of
them.

================================================================
6. SAFELY RESTARTING THE SERVICE
================================================================

A restart is safe by design, but only if you follow the order. Restarting the
coordinator first is fine: it rebuilds its queue from the checkpoints. Restarting
a worker mid-batch is also fine: a batch is only acknowledged after the replica
has durably written it.

The dangerous sequence is restarting a replica while the coordinator still
believes the worker is healthy. The coordinator will keep dispatching batches to
a replica that is shutting down, and the batches will land in the dead-letter
file. To avoid this, drain the worker first, confirm the queue for that worker is
empty, and only then take the replica down.

To restart the whole fleet safely, stop the coordinator, let every worker finish
its in-flight batch, stop the workers, then restart in the reverse order.

================================================================
7. BACKUP AND RESTORE
================================================================

Only the change stream needs a backup. Replica checkpoints are disposable: they
can be rebuilt by replaying the stream, which is slower but always correct.

A restore always replays from the last stream backup. Expect the replay to be
slower than live traffic by a factor of roughly two to three, because it skips
the coordinator's batching optimisation and applies changes one at a time.

Never restore a replica from a filesystem snapshot while the coordinator is
running. The snapshot and the live checkpoint will disagree, and the disagreement
is silent until the next failover.

================================================================
8. SECURITY NOTES
================================================================

The service account can read the change stream and write to replicas. It cannot
read the primary. If you find you need the primary credential to debug a
replication problem, that is a signal the problem is on the primary side, not in
the sync service.

All coordinator-to-worker traffic is mutually authenticated with certificates
that rotate every 90 days. The rotation is automated; the only manual step is
restarting the worker after a rotation if it was offline during the rotation
window.

================================================================
9. WHEN TO ESCALATE
================================================================

Escalate to the platform team when any of the following is true:

  - replication lag has exceeded twenty minutes and is still growing
  - two or more replicas are in the dead-letter state at the same time
  - the change stream read position is advancing but no worker reports progress
  - a restore replay has run for longer than four times the expected duration

Do not escalate a single dead-lettered batch. That is a normal operational
event, and the batch can be replayed once the underlying constraint is fixed.

================================================================
10. GLOSSARY
================================================================

change stream
  The durable, append-only log of every change to the primary datastore. The
  source of truth for replication.

checkpoint
  A per-replica record of the highest sequence number durably applied. Used to
  resume after a restart.

coordinator
  The single process that reads the change stream and dispatches ordered
  batches to workers.

dead-letter file
  Where a worker writes a batch that cannot be applied after exhausting its
  retries, so that it does not block the rest of the stream.

high-water mark
  The queue depth at which the coordinator deliberately stops reading the
  change stream.

replication lag
  The difference between the newest change in the stream and the newest change a
  replica has applied.

worker
  A process that applies batches to exactly one replica.
